Understanding Blepharoplasty
Blepharoplasty is plastic surgery performed on the upper eyelids, lower eyelids or both.
Ageing can affect several structures around the eyes. Skin may become looser, supporting tissues can change and fat may become more noticeable above or below the eyes.
Mayo Clinic describes blepharoplasty as surgery that can address sagging upper eyelid skin, lower eyelid excess skin and bags beneath the eyes. In some patients, severe upper eyelid skin can also interfere with peripheral vision.
Cosmetic eye rejuvenation therefore requires more than deciding that the eye area appears “tired.”
The assessment should determine whether the concern relates primarily to:
excess upper eyelid skin
upper eyelid fat
eyelid asymmetry
lower eyelid bags
loose lower eyelid skin
eyebrow descent
a combination of these factors
a previous eyelid procedure
The correct operation depends on the anatomy creating the concern.
Upper Blepharoplasty
Upper blepharoplasty addresses selected excess tissue of the upper eyelid.
Mayo Clinic describes the typical upper-eyelid approach as an incision positioned along the eyelid fold, allowing selected skin, muscle and potentially fat to be managed before closure.
This is general medical information rather than confirmation of the exact DR. MARIS surgical technique.
Who May Consider Upper Eyelid Surgery?
Upper blepharoplasty may be considered when a patient is concerned about:
loose or redundant upper eyelid skin
heaviness around the upper eyelid
an eyelid crease obscured by excess tissue
asymmetry between the upper eyelids
changes in eyelid shape associated with ageing
a previous eyelid procedure requiring reassessment
In more significant cases, upper eyelid skin can contribute to reduced peripheral vision. That is a functional concern and may require appropriate eye assessment rather than being treated as a purely cosmetic issue.
Upper eyelid surgery should also not automatically be used to treat a low eyebrow.
If brow descent is contributing substantially to upper-eye heaviness, removing additional eyelid skin without recognizing the eyebrow position may not address the underlying anatomical issue.
Lower Blepharoplasty
Lower blepharoplasty addresses selected ageing or contour concerns beneath the eyes.
Common reasons for consultation include:
prominent under-eye bags
visible lower eyelid fat
loose lower eyelid skin
contour irregularity beneath the eyes
asymmetry
changes following previous lower eyelid surgery
Mayo Clinic notes that lower blepharoplasty may use an incision just beneath the eyelashes or an incision inside the lower eyelid, with fat potentially removed or redistributed depending on the surgical plan.
Different lower eyelid problems require different approaches.
A younger patient with prominent fat but little loose skin may require a different operation from a patient with substantial skin laxity.
DR. MARIS should therefore avoid advertising one universal lower-eyelid technique for every patient.
Under-Eye Bags Are Not Always the Same Problem
Patients often describe all lower-eyelid fullness as “eye bags,” but several anatomical factors can influence the appearance of the under-eye region.
These may include:
prominent orbital fat
loose skin
lower-eyelid laxity
differences in cheek volume
fluid retention
facial anatomy
asymmetry
Mayo Clinic specifically notes that blepharoplasty may be an option for some people with under-eye bags but that surgery should be tailored to individual anatomy and needs.
This is why photographs alone should not be used to promise a specific surgical result.
Eyebrow Lift
Some patients believe they need upper eyelid surgery when part of the heaviness comes from a descending eyebrow.
An eyebrow lift is intended to reposition eyebrow tissue rather than primarily remove upper eyelid skin.
The relationship between the eyebrow and upper eyelid is important because the two areas affect one another visually and anatomically.
Professional plastic-surgery guidance distinguishes brow-lifting surgery from blepharoplasty and notes that brow position should be evaluated when upper-eyelid heaviness is present.

Recovery & Aftercare
Early recovery after blepharoplasty commonly involves swelling, bruising, temporary irritation and discomfort.
ASPS notes that dry eyes, irritation and incision-site discomfort can occur during initial healing, with postoperative instructions tailored to the patient.
Mayo Clinic reports that swelling and bruising often decrease progressively over approximately 10–14 days, although scars and subtle tissue changes continue evolving for considerably longer.
This is general medical guidance and not a guaranteed DR. MARIS recovery timeline.
Your recovery may depend on:
upper versus lower blepharoplasty
combined surgery
eyebrow lift
extent of tissue correction
previous eyelid surgery
healing response
underlying eye conditions
Recovery timing depends on the procedure and individual condition and will be discussed during consultation.

What is the difference between upper and lower blepharoplasty?
Upper blepharoplasty addresses selected excess tissue of the upper eyelid, while lower blepharoplasty addresses concerns beneath the eye such as prominent fat bags or loose lower-eyelid skin. The procedures involve different anatomy and should not automatically be combined. Dr. Maris will assess which area is creating the concern before recommending surgery.
Do I need upper blepharoplasty or an eyebrow lift?
It depends on where the heaviness originates. Excess upper-eyelid skin may be addressed with blepharoplasty, while a low eyebrow may require assessment of the brow itself. Some patients have both concerns. Evaluating the eyebrow before removing upper-eyelid skin helps avoid selecting a procedure based solely on appearance in photographs.
Can upper and lower blepharoplasty be performed together?
They can potentially be combined in appropriately assessed patients, but suitability depends on eyelid anatomy, health, eye history and the extent of surgery required. The current DR. MARIS price document lists upper and lower blepharoplasty separately and does not provide an approved combined package price.
Can lower blepharoplasty remove under-eye bags?
Lower blepharoplasty may improve under-eye bags when prominent fat or related lower-eyelid anatomy is contributing to the concern. Mayo Clinic notes that surgery can remove or redistribute excess fat depending on the individual case. Not every under-eye concern is caused by the same structure, so assessment is important.
Will blepharoplasty leave scars?
Blepharoplasty requires surgical incisions, so it should never be described as scarless. Upper-eyelid incisions are commonly positioned within the eyelid fold, while lower-eyelid surgery may use an incision below the lashes or inside the eyelid depending on technique. Scar visibility varies between patients and continues changing during healing.
How long does swelling last after eyelid surgery?
Swelling and bruising generally improve progressively rather than disappearing immediately. Mayo Clinic notes that much of the visible bruising and swelling commonly decreases over about 10–14 days, while scars and subtle tissue changes continue settling for longer. Your actual recovery depends on the operation and individual healing.
Can eyelid surgery cause dry eyes?
Yes. Temporary or persistent eye dryness is a recognized risk of blepharoplasty. Difficulty closing the eyes, irritation and other eyelid-position changes can also occur. Tell Dr. Maris about existing dry-eye symptoms, eye medication or previous ophthalmic conditions before surgery.
How long should I stay in Vietnam after having blepharoplasty?
There is no universal travel period. The recommended stay depends on whether you undergo upper surgery, lower surgery, combined procedures or eyebrow lift, as well as wound healing and postoperative review requirements. Dr. Maris should confirm the appropriate stay before you book your return flight.
Can Dr. Maris assess me before I travel to Vietnam?
Yes. International patients may begin with an online consultation and provide photographs, concerns, medical history and details of previous eyelid surgery where relevant. This supports preliminary planning but does not replace any physical or eye examination required before surgery.
